None reported the race of anyone who killed a driver. That is the same gap T-CLEAR found in the Human Rights Campaign's reports on transgender homicide victims. This study combined those counts, verified every case from 2016 through 2025 against court records, police statements and news coverage, and identified the people charged (N = 164 victims; 157 suspects).

PRINCIPAL FINDINGS

  1. Black suspects account for 78.3% of identified perpetrators with a coded race (101 of 129), 5.76 times the Black share of the US population (13.6%). White suspects account for 9.3% (12 of 129), Hispanic suspects 9.3% and Asian/Other suspects 3.1%. Black suspects are the largest group in every year of the study except 2018, when the only two coded suspects were one Black and one White.
  2. The finding survives the most hostile assumption. If all 28 suspects whose race could not be coded were White, Black suspects would still account for 64.3%, 4.7 times their population share.
  3. Half the killings crossed racial lines. Of 123 suspect–victim pairs with both races coded, 51% are interracial, against roughly 20% in national homicide. Black suspects killed White drivers in 16 pairings; White suspects killed Black drivers in 6.
  4. The passenger is the leading threat. In 59 of the 111 cases where the relationship is documented (53%), the killer was a passenger who summoned the driver through the app. Nearly all of the rest were strangers.
  5. Uber is the most common platform, with 69 of 163 homicide victims, followed by Lyft (45), DoorDash (24) and Uber Eats (10). Rideshare drivers account for about seven of every ten victims.
  6. The killings concentrate in large metro areas. Chicago and Dallas–Fort Worth recorded the most (12 each), followed by Greater Washington, DC (11) and Atlanta (9). By state, Texas recorded the most (20), followed by Illinois (13) and California (12).
  7. 72% of the homicides are solved or charged (118 of 163), and at least 53 have ended in a conviction or sentence. 35 remain unsolved.
  8. Victims are racially diverse: 74 Black, 41 White, 19 Hispanic and 23 Asian/Other, many of them immigrants.

Every case links to its sources, and every suspect's race is labeled with where it came from: a booking, prison or court record, a published photograph, or a news report. Suspects whose race could not be coded are excluded from the race counts rather than assigned one. Photo-based codes are visual classifications, labeled as such, not self-identified race.

Driving strangers for money has been one of the most dangerous jobs in America for a generation. In 1995 the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) reported that taxi drivers had the highest work homicide rate of any occupation, 21 times the national average. A 2017 NIOSH study of 2003 to 2013 found the rate of violent work deaths for taxi and limousine drivers was still far above that of all workers. Then the apps put far more people into the same job, usually with no partition between the driver and a stranger in the back seat.

The government stopped being able to see them. App drivers are classified as independent contractors, so the workplace injury survey and the Occupational Safety and Health Administration leave them out. The one federal death count, the Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries, folds rideshare drivers into the taxi category and cannot tell an Uber driver from a cab driver.

Uber, Lyft, Gig Workers Rising, The Markup each counted part of the toll, and the Government Accountability Office documented why no one else does. Each stopped there. None named a single killer, charge or verdict, and none has a count past early 2023.

This study asks the question they did not: not how many, but who killed them, and what happened afterward.

Finding: 68 of 163 killings (41.7%) were robberies or carjackings. 44 drivers were killed for cash, a phone or a wallet, and 24 for their car. Christina Spicuzza was 38, a mother of four, and drove for Uber around Monroeville, east of Pittsburgh. On the night of February 9, 2022, she picked up a passenger. She never came home. Her body was later found in a wooded area.

She had a rear-facing camera in her car. Prosecutors played about 20 minutes of it in court. It shows the passenger, masked and hooded, sitting silently while she tries to make conversation, then pressing a gun to the back of her head. According to CBS Pittsburgh, she stayed calm and said, “I have four kids, what are you doing?” In the last seconds, he grabbed her phone and pulled the camera down.

The passenger was Calvin Crew, 22, of Pitcairn. He was charged in March 2022. In February 2025 a jury convicted him of first-degree murder, kidnapping and robbery. Prosecutors had dropped the death penalty before trial. In May 2025 he was sentenced to life without parole plus 13 to 26 years. He did not attend his own sentencing. Her family told the court, “You should have the death penalty, but we showed mercy” (AP via AOL).

What the official counts did with her. Uber’s safety report for 2021 and 2022 counts fatal physical assaults on its platform. It names no victim and no attacker, so she is one of 36 numbers. Gig Workers Rising counted the app workers killed in 2022 and called the violence “racialized,” based on the victims’ race. Neither said who killed them.

Her case is not unusual in this dataset. It is the most fully documented. The killer was the passenger. The app matched them. He was charged, convicted and named in court and in the news. That information existed in every one of the 118 homicides solved or charged here, and none of the institutions counting the dead ever put it on a page.

Mohammad Anwar was 66. He came to the United States from Pakistan in 2014, lived in Springfield, Virginia, and delivered food for Uber Eats. His family called him “a hardworking immigrant” who came “to build a better life for himself and his family” (NBC4 Washington).

Around 4:30 p.m. on March 23, 2021, on Van Street SE, a block from Nationals Park, two girls aged 13 and 15 attacked him with a stun gun and tried to take his Honda. Prosecutors said the girls had approached him for a ride near the Navy Yard Metro station (NBC4 Washington). A bystander filmed what came next. Anwar hangs onto the open driver’s door shouting “This is my car!” as the car speeds off. It scrapes a barrier and a light pole, the door slams into him, and the car turns onto N Street and crashes onto its side (FOX 5 DC). He was thrown onto the sidewalk and died at a hospital.

Two teens, ages 13 and 15 are facing felony murder charges after they were filmed in an alleged carjacking gone wrong. Mohammad Anwar, a 66-year-old Pakistani-American immigrant was killed when he tried to stop the attempted carjacking of his car.

Two National Guard troops posted near the stadium pulled the girls out of the overturned car while its wheels were still spinning. As Anwar lay on the sidewalk, one of the girls walked past his body toward the car, yelling for her phone, which was still inside (NZ Herald, describing the video). Prosecutors said the girls also told bystanders the car was theirs (NBC4 Washington). Both were arrested at the scene and charged with felony murder and armed carjacking.

Because they were minors, the case stayed in juvenile court. D.C. law does not allow a 13-year-old to be tried as an adult, and prosecutors did not seek adult charges for the 15-year-old (Wikipedia summary of the court coverage). The 15-year-old pleaded guilty to felony murder in May 2021 and the 13-year-old in June. Each was committed to juvenile custody until age 21, the most the juvenile court could give. At the first sentencing his daughter told the court, “I can never and will never forgive you for the pain you caused him” (NBC4 Washington).

Why this case is here. It does not fit the picture most people have of who kills app drivers. The killers were not grown men. They were two girls, 13 and 15, both Black, armed with a stun gun. The man who died clinging to his car door was a 66-year-old Pakistani grandfather. The video aired across the country. Still, none of the counts of app workers killed on the job ever said who killed him.

This study found almost all of them through local news, court records and prosecutors’ filings. Ask yourself how many of their names you have heard. Then ask how many of the three stories above you remember.

Why this matters. Passenger harassment was wall-to-wall news. A driver’s murder got a minute on local TV. The attention was not wrong. A slur or an assault on a driver deserves a response. The problem is proportion. Incidents that fit the story of the racist passenger were filmed, shared, and condemned by the companies, a district attorney and national networks. The killings, as far as this study could find, were reported almost entirely by local outlets, including the ones that cut against that story: in this study, Black suspects killed White drivers in 16 pairings, and White suspects killed Black drivers in 6. If one slur caught on camera is national news, a driver shot dead on the job should be too.

Tap any driver to open the news report on the killing. Every case is in its year page.

This study contains two distinct layers.

The empirical layer (Sections I–VII) is independent case-level data collection: every documented killing located, verified against local reporting, police statements, prosecutors' offices and court records, then coded across sixteen fields and benchmarked against the counts published by Uber, Lyft, Gig Workers Rising and the federal government. Every data point is sourced, every coding decision documented, every limitation stated.

Section VIII names the suspects. That is the part no one else did. Uber and Lyft published aggregate fatal-assault totals without naming a victim or a perpetrator. An advocacy group counted 80 dead and attributed the toll to platform design without documenting a single killer. A federal report concluded no one is required to collect the data at all. Those are factual claims with policy consequences, and they can be tested against a case-level record. This study tests them against the named, case-level record.

Research Paper: Sections V–XV
Click any section to expand. Sections V to XV follow the order of a scientific paper: literature review, methodology, statistical results, the suspects and victims, who blamed racism, objections, discussion, conclusion, references, and acknowledgments.

Gig Workers Rising. The only sustained advocacy count. Its April 2022 report documented more than 50 app workers killed between 2017 and January 2022 and found 63% were people of color. Its follow-up, Murdered Behind the Wheel, raised the total to 80 across 2017 through 2022, with at least 31 killed in 2022 alone, 77% of them people of color and 26% immigrants. The method was a review of press reports, police reports, and court records. The 2022 victims were named in a memorial list. No perpetrator was named in any year. The group described its own tally as not comprehensive and likely to exclude many workers, and its public database stops on April 15, 2023.

The Markup. The only newsroom to build an original dataset. Reporter Dara Kerr's Working for an Algorithm series documented 124 carjackings and attempted carjackings of rideshare drivers over roughly eighteen months in 2021, then 361 such incidents since 2017 with 28 workers killed by July 2022, publishing the underlying data openly. The series drew on driver interviews, probable cause statements, public records requests, and court filings. Systematic tracking stopped in 2022. The newsroom was acquired by CalMatters in 2024 and continues publishing.

Uber. Three US Safety Reports covering 2017 through 2022. Fatal physical assaults: 19, then 20, then 36, with the driver share of fatalities reaching 61% in the 2021 to 2022 edition. The counting rule matters as much as the count. Uber classifies an assault as platform-related only when it involves a party on an app-facilitated trip or occurs between app-paired parties within 48 hours of that trip. A driver killed while logged on but between fares, or killed by someone the app never paired them with, can fall outside that definition entirely. No victim is named in any edition. No report has been published covering 2023 through 2025.

Lyft. Two Community Safety Reports: 10 fatal physical assaults across 2017 through 2019, and 23 across 2020 through 2022. Lyft's definition is broader than Uber's in one respect, since it says it will include an incident even where it occurred after a completed trip or cannot be matched to a ride. No victim is named. Nothing has been published since July 2024.

Every other platform. DoorDash, Instacart, Grubhub, Amazon Flex, GoPuff, and Postmates have never published a safety report or any fatality data. Delivery workers appear repeatedly among the dead in this dataset and in Gig Workers Rising's, and their employers have published nothing.

The federal government. GAO's 2024 report and its 2026 follow-up, both mandated by Sami's Law, are the authoritative account of the gap: no federal collection requirement, fragmentary coverage across six databases, an occupational code that merges rideshare with taxi, and the exclusion of self-employed workers from the two systems most likely to capture workplace violence. The Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries recorded 19 fatal intentional-injury deaths across the combined taxi and ridesourcing industry for 2019. Among state regulators, only the California Public Utilities Commission routinely collects incident data from these companies.

Litigation. The largest disclosures came from discovery, not from any tracking system. A 2022 filing in a federal wrongful death case indicated Uber had produced at least 24,000 safety incident reports involving physical assaults on drivers by passengers between 2017 and 2020. In 2025, reporting on the consolidated sexual assault litigation put the number of sexual assault and misconduct reports Uber received between 2017 and 2022 at 400,181, against the roughly 12,500 serious assaults the company had published. Both figures concern assaults rather than homicides, and both emerged under seal. They establish the scale of what platforms hold and do not publish.

One further compilation, noted rather than relied on. The only running list covering the years before 2017 was maintained by the taxi industry as an advocacy campaign against rideshare, catalogued rideshare incidents exclusively and never taxi incidents, and stopped publishing around 2018. Its totals were never independently verifiable and no figure from it is used anywhere in this project.

Academic research. Colleges and federal researchers have studied this job from two directions, and neither looked at who kills drivers. The first is occupational safety. NIOSH found in the early 1990s that taxi drivers had the highest work homicide rate of any occupation, and a 2013 NIOSH study in the American Journal of Preventive Medicine (Menéndez and colleagues) found that cities requiring security cameras in cabs had about one seventh the driver homicide rate they had before. In 2025 Washington State’s fatality investigators issued a hazard alert on six rideshare and delivery driver homicides from 2020 to 2024, recommending cameras and alarms. These studies count deaths and test equipment. None describes the people who did the killing.

The second direction is discrimination by drivers. A 2016 field experiment by Ge, Knittel, MacKenzie and Zoepf (MIT, Stanford and the University of Washington), published in the Journal of Public Economics in 2020, ran about 1,500 rides in Seattle and Boston and found longer waits and more cancellations for Black riders. A 2024 study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences by Cobb and colleagues at Carnegie Mellon found the apps reduce that wait penalty to seconds, while residential segregation still produces longer waits. This is the best-funded academic work on ride-hailing and race, and it measures what drivers do to passengers. No academic study found here measures what passengers and strangers did to drivers, or who they were.

The occupational literature. Driving for hire has been documented as one of the deadliest jobs in America for thirty years. NIOSH found taxicab drivers had the highest occupational homicide rate of any occupation in the early 1990s, roughly 22.7 per 100,000, and later documented 366 violent work deaths among taxi and limousine drivers between 2003 and 2013. That literature is the closest available analogue, and it now silently absorbs rideshare drivers into the same occupational category, which is precisely why they cannot be counted separately.

The gap this study fills: no institutional effort has maintained a continuous named list past April 2023, and none has documented the perpetrators. The one outside exception is National Conservative, a site that since November 2023 has kept a running list of app-driver killings with known suspects, now covering 2021 through 2026; it was used here as a cross-check (see Acknowledgments). For 2024 the two lists match exactly. The claim tested throughout this project is narrow and specific: not that nobody counted, but that none of the institutions counting ever identified who was doing the killing, or what happened to them afterward.

Study period. 2016 through 2025, ten complete calendar years. 2026 is deferred until the year closes.

Inclusion criteria. Homicide of a driver working for an app-based rideshare or delivery platform at the time of the killing, or killed in direct connection with that work. Amazon Flex and all delivery apps are in scope. Killings that occurred while the driver was working but were not motivated by the work are included motive-blind, with circumstances stated, matching the standard used by the institutional list, which also counts drivers killed immediately after a final trip. Non-criminal traffic accidents are excluded. Police-involved killings of drivers are counted in victim totals and broken out of all homicide statistics; the decade documents one, in 2023.

Case rules. (1) Intentional killings only. Deaths caused by drunk drivers, police chases, hit-and-runs and similar crashes are excluded: Henry O. Reyes, Thor Anderson, Folesha Parker-Iverson, Bismark Asare, Mohamed Kamara, Jennifer Kelleher, Alex Habib, El Moctar Sidiya, Nicole Snell, Ibrahim Diakite. Killings during a carjacking or by a passenger who ran the driver over stay in. (2) POSSIBLE cases are listed but not counted when no app is named, no one was charged, or there is no homicide ruling: Delano Deonte Jackson (2016, drove for Uber, but no source says he was working when killed); Hamza Jamil Zaki Abo Halaoh (2023, no app named; his employer described a company “like Uber”); Christian Estrada-Petersen (2024, family did not know if he was working for Uber when shot); Michael Wallace (2018, no charge and no homicide ruling); Randolph Tolk (2017, charge dropped; DA said death was medical); Qiaoming Shao (2021, no app named); Brandon Dean (2024, no app named); Aaron Wiley (2018, no app named; unsolved). (3) Off-shift drivers count only when the killing came shortly after a shift. (4) Rossana Delgado (2021) is excluded: Lyft and the Georgia Bureau of Investigation found no evidence she drove for Lyft, and local reporting describes her as a taxi driver. (5) Suspects counted are people charged with the killing, plus killers identified by police who died before they could be charged (four people). People who were acquitted, had charges dismissed, pleaded only to a non-homicide charge, were charged only as accessories, or were charged in a different killing are shown but not counted. (6) Every year table has a Race source column showing whether each race came from a booking, prison or court record, a published photo, or a news report.

Independent audit, November 2026. An independent audit reproduced this study’s totals and confidence intervals and found that the Black majority among coded suspects survives every correction it could substantiate. It also found real problems, now fixed: three victims whose app work at the time was never confirmed (Delano Deonte Jackson, 2016; Hamza Jamil Zaki Abo Halaoh, 2023; Christian Estrada-Petersen, 2024) moved to possible cases; the two girls charged in Mohammad Anwar’s killing are now counted as two suspects rather than one charging entry; three race calls made without any record or photo were recoded as undetermined; Oluwatosin Adetula’s case was recoded from Uber to Lyft to match the charging documents; and binomial p-values against national population shares were replaced with confidence intervals and plain context. Totals moved from 167 victims and 156 suspects to 164 and 157, and the Black share of coded suspects moved from 77.9% to 78.3%. Of the 129 coded suspects, 40 rest on a record or news report that states race, 83 on the author’s reading of a published photo or video, and 6 on other author judgment. The full data is downloadable, one row per person: suspects (CSV) and victims (CSV), each with its race source and source tier. Each row now carries its source URLs. Using only the 40 suspects whose race rests on a record or news report, 31 are Black (77.5%); using only the 83 coded from a photo or video, 66 are Black (79.5%). The finding does not depend on the photo coding. A follow-up review confirmed these fixes and flagged smaller ones, also fixed: stale metro counts in the findings list, the denominator for the share of minors, wording that said “killed” beside people only charged, and a caveat on what the confidence intervals leave out. The two codes that rested only on the first version of this study (Devin Powell and Kemarrie Davion Phillips) were confirmed in November 2026 against Indiana and Michigan Department of Correction records, which list both as Black. One item remains open and is stated here so readers can weigh it: no second person has independently re-coded the race calls.

Source provenance, stated plainly because it bears on independence. Coverage falls into three eras. For 2016 no institutional list exists, and the roster was assembled from contemporaneous local reporting and court records. For 2017 through April 2023 the victim list is anchored to Gig Workers Rising's report and public database, then independently verified case by case, with cases their list missed added. For mid-2023 through 2025 no institutional list exists. The roster was compiled from local news, police statements, prosecutors' offices, and court records, then checked against National Conservative’s running list; for 2024 the two lists contain the same 21 cases. This means the 2017 to 2022 totals are not fully independent of Gig Workers Rising: this study's 88 homicides for that window and their 80 app-worker murders share a starting point and should not be read as two independent counts. Comparisons against Uber's and Lyft's published figures are independent of those companies, since no platform data was used to build the roster.

Verification. Every case was checked against local news coverage, police press releases, prosecutors' announcements, and court records where accessible, for victim identity, platform, manner of death, killer identity, relationship, and case status. Institutional characterizations and framing were not used as data inputs.

Coding framework. Suspect race comes from a booking, prison or court record, a published photo, or a news report, and every person’s race source is shown on the year pages. Where the author judged race from a photo without a record, the source reads “Author call”; calls marked “assumed” were made without a photo or record. Surnames are never treated as documentation, and anyone whose race could not be coded is left uncoded. Race sources differ in strength and are labeled so readers can weigh them: a record or news report that states race is strongest; a published photo judged by the author is a visual classification; an author call is weakest. After the November 2026 audit, three calls made without any record or photo (Da’Trayvon Mitchell, Tylan Peaks and Alex Waggoner) were recoded as undetermined. Relationship between killer and victim is documented-only, with values including Passenger, Stranger with the mechanism stated, Ex-partner, and Not documented. Unsolved cases always code Unknown regardless of any suspected mechanism. Case status distinguishes Convicted or Sentenced, Charged, Closed where the identified killer died before charges, Unsolved, and cases where police identified a person and no charging decision was ever publicly reported. Bias motive is not coded for this dataset. Age is taken as published at the time of the killing.

What Solved or Charged means here. Any homicide with at least one person convicted of or charged with the killing. Cases closed because the killer died are counted separately, not folded in. Two trial acquittals and one case with an identified but never charged killer fall in neither the solved nor the unsolved column.

Stated limitations. No population, driver-headcount, or trip-volume denominator exists publicly, so no rate per driver, per trip, or per capita is computed anywhere in this project, and none should be inferred from these counts. A state or platform with more cases may simply have more drivers. The absence of a case in a given state means none was documented, not that none occurred. Every total is a floor, and the floors are thinnest in the earliest years and after April 2023, where no institutional list exists to check against. 28 of the 157 counted suspects (18%) have no coded race, so every racial breakdown of suspects describes the 129 with a coded race. This study was conducted by a single researcher without formal inter-rater reliability testing. Most race codes rest on a booking, prison or court record or a published photo; a minority are the author’s judgment from other published images, and each one is labeled with its source.

Reproducibility. All case data derives from publicly accessible sources, and every case on every year page carries its source links. Any researcher with access to court records and local news archives can verify every data point.

The most useful statistical test available for this dataset is not a significance test against population shares. It is a reconciliation against the other bodies that counted, because each used a different method and each published a number.

Kamala Harris and implicit bias training. In November 2015, then-California Attorney General Kamala Harris launched the nation’s first-of-its-kind implicit bias training program for law enforcement, a POST-certified course developed in partnership with Stanford University. The program’s premise was that racial disparities in arrest data reflected unconscious police bias, and that training officers to recognize those biases would narrow the gaps. As Attorney General from 2011 to 2017, Harris had direct access to every annual Crime in California report published during her tenure. Every one of those reports showed the same pattern: Hispanic individuals leading homicide arrests, Black individuals second at over four times their population share, White individuals underrepresented. This data was compiled by her own office.

The training did not produce the expected result. Hispanic individuals led California homicide arrests before the program (2000–2014), during it (2015–2016), and for the entire decade after it (2017–2024). The rank order never shifted. The ratios never moved. Twenty-four consecutive years of data, spanning five Democratic attorneys general, producing the identical pattern before, during, and after the most high-profile implicit bias intervention in the country.

The data suggests the training addressed the wrong variable. If the arrest disparities had been a product of officer bias, a statewide bias-reduction program should have produced measurable change. It did not. The simplest explanation consistent with 24 years of unchanging data is that the arrest patterns were reflecting actual offending patterns, not police discretion. Harris’s own office published the evidence every year. The pattern did not change because the underlying reality had not changed.

OBJECTION 8“Prison Demographics Show More White Inmates Than Black”▼

The claim: A viral video by Garrison Hayes (@garrisonh, 357K followers) argues that more White people are in prison than Black, citing Bureau of Prisons statistics showing “White” as the largest racial category in federal custody. The claim is wrong. The BOP publishes race and ethnicity on two separate pages, and the “White” count on the race page absorbs all Hispanic inmates into the White category. When you subtract Hispanic inmates using the BOP’s own ethnicity page, White Americans are the third-largest group in federal prison, not the first.

The Two-Page Problem: How BOP Reports Race and Ethnicity

The Bureau of Prisons maintains two separate statistics pages. The Race page reports inmates as White, Black, Native American, or Asian. The Ethnicity page separately reports how many inmates are Hispanic. Because Hispanic is treated as an ethnicity and not a race, every Hispanic inmate is also counted as “White” (or another race) on the race page. Anyone who cites only the race page without adjusting for the ethnicity page is double-counting Hispanic inmates as White.

The math (BOP, February 28, 2026 · 152,780 total inmates):
BOP Race page lists “White” inmates: 87,024 (57.0%)
BOP Ethnicity page lists Hispanic inmates: 45,109 (29.5%)
87,024 − 45,109 = 41,915 actual non-Hispanic White inmates (27.4%)

The “White” number on the race page is inflated by 107% due to Hispanic absorption. The real non-Hispanic White share is less than half of what the race page shows.

The claim and its refutation. Garrison Hayes cited BOP statistics showing “White” as the largest racial group in federal prison. This is a classification artifact, not a demographic reality. The BOP’s race page counts every Hispanic inmate as “White,” inflating the White count by 107%. When the BOP’s own ethnicity data is applied, non-Hispanic White Americans drop from an apparent 57% to an actual 27.4% of federal inmates, ranking third behind Black (38.4%) and Hispanic (29.5%). The BJS state-plus-federal data, which properly separates ethnicity, confirms Black as the largest group at 33% of all state and federal prisoners, from approximately 13% of the population.

This is the same classification problem shown in Objection 3. The FBI absorbs Hispanic offenders into “White” in its UCR data. The BOP does the same with its race page. In both cases, the result is the same: the White share is artificially inflated, and anyone who cites the uncorrected number is either unaware of the classification methodology or unconcerned with its distortive effect.

The dispatch is the mechanism. In 59 of the 111 homicides where the relationship is documented, the killer was the passenger on that trip. In those cases the app did not fail to stop a random crime. It did its job: it matched two strangers and sent one to the other. Whether that makes the platforms liable is a legal question this study does not answer. It does mean the risk comes with the product, not just the neighborhood.

Half of these killings crossed racial lines. 51% of the 123 pairings with both races coded were interracial, against roughly 20% in national homicide. Most homicide happens between people who live near each other. App driving breaks that pattern by design: it sends a workforce that is heavily immigrant into neighborhoods its drivers do not live in. The nearly 18-year gap between the average victim (42) and the average suspect (25) points the same way. These were not neighbors killing neighbors.

The research looked one way. Over the same decade, field experiments, a PNAS paper, federal attention and steady press coverage measured whether drivers discriminated against Black passengers. Nobody measured who was killing drivers. One side of the ledger was funded, peer-reviewed and covered. The other produced 164 dead and no list of suspects.

What the numbers say, and what they do not. Of the 129 suspects with a coded race, 101 are Black: 78.3%, 5.8 times the Black share of the population. That holds under every confidence interval in Section VII, every assumption in Objection 1, and an independent audit that recoded the weakest calls. The same data does not support screening passengers by race. Uber reported about 2.1 billion U.S. trips in 2019 and 2020 combined against five driver deaths from assault in its own report, roughly one in 400 million trips. No rule keyed to a passenger’s race can be justified as risk management at that rate.

The real policy question is penalties. Drivers report finishing rides they felt were unsafe because cancellations and low acceptance rates cost them. In June 2022, members of Congress wrote to Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Grubhub and Instacart saying no driver should be punished for declining a ride or delivery they believe is dangerous. Uber says a safety cancellation at pickup does not count against a driver, which does not cover acceptance rates or declines before pickup. The question this record raises is whether a driver’s judgment about a specific situation should cost them anything.

The gaps are findings too. 28 of 157 suspects have no coded race, mostly juveniles with sealed records and people never photographed. 35 homicides are unsolved. No race is assigned to any of them, and published lists that assign one in either direction are guessing.

The people who drive America to work and bring it dinner are part of the backbone of its working economy. At least 164 app-based drivers were killed working in the United States between 2016 and 2025, 163 of them in homicides. 35 have never been solved. In 59 of the 111 cases where the relationship is documented, the person who killed the driver arrived because the app sent them. The average victim was 42, and many were immigrants. The average suspect was 25, and one in five with a known age was a minor. Of the 129 suspects whose race could be coded, 101 were Black.

For ten years, these workers were told what to fear, and it was not this. Uber told its riders, “If you tolerate racism, delete Uber.” Labor advocates warned of drivers of color threatened by white passengers. Gig Workers Rising called the killing of drivers “racialized” and reported the race of the dead. Researchers caught drivers cancelling on riders with Black-sounding names, and Uber answered that discrimination had no place on its app. Not one of them asked who was killing the drivers. Black suspects killed White drivers in 16 pairings; White suspects killed Black drivers in 7.

Gig Workers Rising did for app drivers what the Human Rights Campaign (HRC) did for transgender victims in the companion T-CLEAR study: it counted the dead, named them, and never looked at the people who killed them. Uber and Lyft counted their own dead drivers and named no one. The federal government cannot separate these workers from taxi drivers in its own data and is not required to try. Every one of those counts was defensible on its own terms, and together they left the central question of every case unanswered for a decade.

The drivers were treated as the problem. The record says their wariness was not invented. It does not say any one rider is dangerous: 163 killings across ten years and billions of trips is a small risk on any single ride, and refusing a rider because of race is illegal and wrong. But a workforce warned about the wrong threat for a decade was owed the truth about the right one.

And this is only the killings. This study does not count the robberies, the beatings, the shootings drivers survived, or the sexual assaults. Uber’s own U.S. safety reports logged about 6,000 sexual assault reports in 2017 and 2018 and 998 in 2020 alone, and riders were the accused in 43 to 45 percent of them (ABC7 Chicago). Neither company has ever published who the accused were. Those records exist, and someone should ask for them.

The record of the killings now exists. It is incomplete and every count in it is a floor, and corrections are welcome. It is also the most complete public account yet of who did it. The drivers were owed that question ten years ago.

Methodology summary: This index aggregates ten year datasets; every count is computed from the case tables on those pages, and each year page documents its sources case by case. Source coverage falls in three eras: 2016, assembled from contemporaneous local reporting and court records with no institutional list available; 2017 through April 2023, anchored to Gig Workers Rising's Murdered Behind the Wheel database and independently verified case by case; and mid-2023 through 2025, compiled directly from local news, police statements, prosecutors' offices, and court records. Inclusion: homicide of a driver working for an app-based rideshare or delivery platform, Amazon Flex included, at the time of the killing or in direct connection with that work; killings while working but not because of the work are included motive-blind with circumstances stated; non-criminal traffic accidents excluded. Police-involved killings are counted in victim totals and broken out of homicide statistics. Suspect race comes from records, published photos or news reports, with the source shown for every person; anyone whose race could not be coded is left uncoded. Relationship coding is documented-only and unsolved cases always code Unknown. Solved or Charged counts any homicide with at least one person convicted or charged; cases closed because the killer died are counted separately. No rate per driver, per trip, or per capita is computed anywhere in this project, because no public source records how many app drivers work in a given place or year.
