Background. Between 2016 and 2025 at least 151 app-based rideshare and delivery drivers were killed working in the United States. Four bodies produced partial counts of that toll. Uber's US Safety Reports record fatal physical assaults on its platform: 19 across 2017-2018, 20 across 2019-2020, and 36 across 2021-2022, with the driver share reaching 61% in the final edition. Lyft's Community Safety Reports record 10 across 2017-2019 and 23 across 2020-2022. The advocacy group Gig Workers Rising counted 80 app workers murdered between 2017 and 2022 and stopped updating on April 15, 2023. The Government Accountability Office, reporting to Congress under Sami's Law, found no federal requirement to collect this data at all. Every one of those counts is anonymous, and none names a single perpetrator.

Objective. To construct the first continuous, named, case-level record of these killings, and to test whether the assumptions embedded in the existing counts survive contact with the case files.

Method. Every documented homicide of an app-based driver from 2016 through 2025, verified case by case against local news reporting, police statements, prosecutors' offices, and court records. Sixteen fields per case, including the killer's identity where charged, the relationship between killer and driver, the charge, and the outcome. Where a fact is not publicly documented it is coded unknown; nothing is inferred.

THE QUESTION, AND WHAT THE DATA ANSWERED

The question. Everything written about race and ride-hailing concerns what drivers do to passengers: field experiments, a PNAS paper, federal attention, a decade of press, all measuring how often drivers cancel on Black riders. Nobody asked the inverse. Over the same decade at least 151 drivers were killed on the job and no institution recorded who killed them. This study asks only that: who is killing app drivers, and what happened afterward.

Who kills app drivers?
148 people have been charged. 75 of them (50.7%) have no publicly documented race, mostly juveniles with sealed records and suspects still at large. Of the 73 who do: 57 Black, 7 White, 7 Hispanic, 2 Middle Eastern. Every racial figure here covers half the charged population. This study assigns race to no one.
How does the killer reach the driver?
In 53 of the 108 cases with a documented relationship, the killer was a passenger on the fatal trip. The app matched them and sent the driver to the address. The other 52 were strangers: carjackings, robberies, ambushes, road rage.
Who dies?
Mean age 41.9. An immigrant workforce, and the dead reflect it: 59 Black, 30 White, 19 Hispanic, 15 Asian or Pacific Islander, 9 Middle Eastern, from at least fourteen countries of origin.
Do killer and victim come from the same community?
Usually not. 60% of documented pairings cross racial lines, against roughly 20% nationally. Dispatch is why: drivers are sent into neighbourhoods they do not live in, so these are not encounters between neighbours.
How old are the people charged?
Mean age 24.6, seventeen years younger than the drivers they killed, in every year of the decade. 28 of 143 were minors.
Does anyone get caught?
108 of 150 (72.0%) have someone convicted or charged. 32 are unsolved, 7 closed with the killer dead. Clearance barely moves by victim race: Black 74.1%, White 73.3%, Hispanic 73.7%.
Were the companies hiding the toll?
No. This count reproduces Uber's own published figure exactly for 2019-2020, 5 against 5. The numbers were never secret. The names were.

Conclusion. The counts were never the hard part. Uber knew its driver death toll to the case; Lyft knew; an advocacy group with no institutional support built a defensible list from public records. What none of them produced, and what required going back through ten years of court files case by case, is the other half of every one of these events: who did it, what they were charged with, and what a court did about it.

Roughly a decade ago, a category of work appeared that had no precedent in American labor statistics: millions of people driving strangers and packages for money, dispatched by algorithm, classified as independent contractors, and therefore invisible to nearly every system built to track what happens to workers.

The consequences of that invisibility are administrative and specific. Because app drivers are independent contractors, they fall outside the Bureau of Labor Statistics Survey of Occupational Injuries and Illnesses and outside the Occupational Safety and Health Administration's information system. The Government Accountability Office reported that roughly 89% of taxi and rideshare drivers were self-employed as of 2021. The one federal count that does include the self-employed, the Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries, uses an occupation code that cannot distinguish an Uber driver from a taxi driver, and suppresses small counts. When GAO reviewed the field for Congress, it found six federal databases holding fragments and no requirement that anyone collect the data at all.

Into that vacuum came three partial counts. Uber and Lyft began publishing safety reports containing aggregate fatal assault totals. Gig Workers Rising, a driver advocacy group, assembled a list of murdered app workers from press and court records. The Markup, a nonprofit newsroom, built an original dataset of carjackings and killings. All three were real work. All three counted victims and stopped there. None documented a single perpetrator, charge, or verdict. Two of the three stopped publishing entirely, and the companies have released nothing covering 2023 through 2025.

This study asks the question none of them asked. Not how many, but who, and what happened afterward. It documents every case from 2016 through 2025, identifies the people charged, records the relationship between killer and driver, and tracks each case to its outcome or to its status as unsolved.

The result is not an indictment of the existing counts. Where comparison is possible, they hold up. It is an account of what a count leaves out when it stops at the body.

Reading these rows: "Solve Rate" counts homicides with at least one person convicted or charged, so it excludes cases closed because the killer died before charges, which is why a few year pages publish a slightly higher figure using their own convention. "Race Coded" counts charged individuals whose race is publicly documented, not the number of suspects. The mini bars show documented victim race only, so their totals fall short of the year's victim count wherever race was never published.

This study contains two distinct layers.

The empirical layer (Sections I–V and VIII) is independent case-level data collection: every documented killing located, verified against local reporting, police statements, prosecutors' offices and court records, then coded across sixteen fields and benchmarked against the counts published by Uber, Lyft, Gig Workers Rising and the federal government. Every data point is sourced, every coding decision documented, every limitation stated.

The responsive layer (Sections VI–VII and the robustness checks) exists because specific, testable claims were made about this population by organisations that never examined it at the case level. Uber and Lyft published aggregate fatal-assault totals without naming a victim or a perpetrator. An advocacy group counted 80 dead and attributed the toll to platform design without documenting a single killer. A federal report concluded no one is required to collect the data at all. Those are factual claims with policy consequences, and they can be tested against a case-level record. The responsive sections do exactly that, using the same dataset and the same sourcing standard as the empirical layer.

Readers who want only the evidence should read Sections I through V and VIII. Readers who want the argument built on it should continue into VI and VII, where the interpretation is labelled as interpretation.

Gig Workers Rising. The only sustained advocacy count. Its April 2022 report documented more than 50 app workers killed between 2017 and January 2022 and found 63% were people of color. Its follow-up, Murdered Behind the Wheel, raised the total to 80 across 2017 through 2022, with at least 31 killed in 2022 alone, 77% of them people of color and 26% immigrants. The method was a review of press reports, police reports, and court records. The 2022 victims were named in a memorial list. No perpetrator was named in any year. The group described its own tally as not comprehensive and likely to exclude many workers, and its public database stops on April 15, 2023.

The Markup. The only newsroom to build an original dataset. Reporter Dara Kerr's Working for an Algorithm series documented 124 carjackings and attempted carjackings of rideshare drivers over roughly eighteen months in 2021, then 361 such incidents since 2017 with 28 workers killed by July 2022, publishing the underlying data openly. The series drew on driver interviews, probable cause statements, public records requests, and court filings. Systematic tracking stopped in 2022. The newsroom was acquired by CalMatters in 2024 and continues publishing.

Uber. Three US Safety Reports covering 2017 through 2022. Fatal physical assaults: 19, then 20, then 36, with the driver share of fatalities reaching 61% in the 2021 to 2022 edition. The counting rule matters as much as the count. Uber classifies an assault as platform-related only when it involves a party on an app-facilitated trip or occurs between app-paired parties within 48 hours of that trip. A driver killed while logged on but between fares, or killed by someone the app never paired them with, can fall outside that definition entirely. No victim is named in any edition. No report has been published covering 2023 through 2025.

Lyft. Two Community Safety Reports: 10 fatal physical assaults across 2017 through 2019, and 23 across 2020 through 2022. Lyft's definition is broader than Uber's in one respect, since it says it will include an incident even where it occurred after a completed trip or cannot be matched to a ride. No victim is named. Nothing has been published since July 2024.

Every other platform. DoorDash, Instacart, Grubhub, Amazon Flex, GoPuff, and Postmates have never published a safety report or any fatality data. Delivery workers appear repeatedly among the dead in this dataset and in Gig Workers Rising's, and their employers have published nothing.

The federal government. GAO's 2024 report and its 2026 follow-up, both mandated by Sami's Law, are the authoritative account of the gap: no federal collection requirement, fragmentary coverage across six databases, an occupational code that merges rideshare with taxi, and the exclusion of self-employed workers from the two systems most likely to capture workplace violence. The Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries recorded 19 fatal intentional-injury deaths across the combined taxi and ridesourcing industry for 2019. Among state regulators, only the California Public Utilities Commission routinely collects incident data from these companies.

Litigation. The largest disclosures came from discovery, not from any tracking system. A 2022 filing in a federal wrongful death case indicated Uber had produced at least 24,000 safety incident reports involving physical assaults on drivers by passengers between 2017 and 2020. In 2025, reporting on the consolidated sexual assault litigation put the number of sexual assault and misconduct reports Uber received between 2017 and 2022 at 400,181, against the roughly 12,500 serious assaults the company had published. Both figures concern assaults rather than homicides, and both emerged under seal. They establish the scale of what platforms hold and do not publish.

One further compilation, noted rather than relied on. The only running list covering the years before 2017 was maintained by the taxi industry as an advocacy campaign against rideshare, catalogued rideshare incidents exclusively and never taxi incidents, and stopped publishing around 2018. Its totals were never independently verifiable and no figure from it is used anywhere in this project.

The occupational literature. Driving for hire has been documented as one of the deadliest jobs in America for thirty years. NIOSH found taxicab drivers had the highest occupational homicide rate of any occupation in the early 1990s, roughly 22.7 per 100,000, and later documented 366 violent work deaths among taxi and limousine drivers between 2003 and 2013. That literature is the closest available analogue, and it now silently absorbs rideshare drivers into the same occupational category, which is precisely why they cannot be counted separately.

The gap this study fills: no prior effort has maintained a continuous named list past April 2023, and no prior effort in any period has documented the perpetrators. The claim tested throughout this project is narrow and specific: not that nobody counted, but that nobody ever identified who was doing the killing, or what happened to them afterward.

Study period. 2016 through 2025, ten complete calendar years. 2026 is deferred until the year closes.

Inclusion criteria. Homicide of a driver working for an app-based rideshare or delivery platform at the time of the killing, or killed in direct connection with that work. Amazon Flex and all delivery apps are in scope. Killings that occurred while the driver was working but were not motivated by the work are included motive-blind, with circumstances stated, matching the standard used by the institutional list, which also counts drivers killed immediately after a final trip. Non-criminal traffic accidents are excluded. Police-involved killings of drivers are counted in victim totals and broken out of all homicide statistics; the decade documents one, in 2023.

Source provenance, stated plainly because it bears on independence. Coverage falls into three eras. For 2016 no institutional list exists, and the roster was assembled from contemporaneous local reporting and court records. For 2017 through April 2023 the victim list is anchored to Gig Workers Rising's report and public database, then independently verified case by case and expanded with cases they missed. For mid-2023 through 2025 no institutional list exists, and the roster was compiled directly from local news, police statements, prosecutors' offices, and court records. This means the 2017 to 2022 totals are not fully independent of Gig Workers Rising, and the convergence between this study's 80 homicides for that window and their 80 app-worker murders should not be read as two independent counts arriving at the same answer. Comparisons against Uber's and Lyft's published figures are independent of those companies, since no platform data was used to build the roster.

Verification. Every case was checked against local news coverage, police press releases, prosecutors' announcements, and court records where accessible, for victim identity, platform, manner of death, killer identity, relationship, and case status. Institutional characterizations and framing were not used as data inputs.

Coding framework. Suspect race is recorded only where publicly documented through a booking photo, court imagery, or a law enforcement description. Unknowns are coded unknown and never inferred. Neither surnames nor complexion descriptions are treated as documentation. Codings marked provisional on the year pages await a final photo verification pass. Relationship between killer and victim is documented-only, with values including Passenger, Stranger with the mechanism stated, Ex-partner, and Not documented. Unsolved cases always code Unknown regardless of any suspected mechanism. Case status distinguishes Convicted or Sentenced, Charged, Closed where the identified killer died before charges, Unsolved, and cases where police identified a person and no charging decision was ever publicly reported. Bias motive is not coded for this dataset. Age is taken as published at the time of the killing.

What Solved or Charged means here. Any homicide with at least one person convicted of or charged with the killing. Cases closed because the killer died are counted separately, not folded in. One 2017 acquittal and two cases with an identified but never charged shooter fall in neither the solved nor the unsolved column.

Stated limitations. No population, driver-headcount, or trip-volume denominator exists publicly, so no rate per driver, per trip, or per capita is computed anywhere in this project, and none should be inferred from these counts. A state or platform with more cases may simply have more drivers. The absence of a case in a given state means none was documented, not that none occurred. Every total is a floor, and the floors are thinnest in the earliest years and after April 2023, where no institutional list exists to check against. Half of all charged individuals have no documented race, so every racial breakdown of suspects describes the documented half only. This study was conducted by a single researcher without formal inter-rater reliability testing, though race coding relies on published imagery rather than subjective assessment.

Reproducibility. All case data derives from publicly accessible sources, and every case on every year page carries its source links. Any researcher with access to court records and local news archives can verify every data point.

The most useful statistical test available for this dataset is not a significance test against population shares. It is a reconciliation against the three other bodies that counted, because each used a different method and each published a number.

Three observations follow. First, the platform comparisons are close, and they are independent, since no platform data entered this roster. An independently assembled case list built from local news and court records reproduces Uber's internal driver-death count for 2019 and 2020 exactly, and falls modestly short for 2021 and 2022, which is the expected direction given that a public-records method will miss cases a company's own incident system captures.

Second, the Gig Workers Rising comparisons look striking and must not be oversold. Those totals share provenance, since the 2017 through April 2023 roster is anchored to their database. The identical totals are partly an artifact of that shared starting point, and identical totals do not mean identical case lists in any event, since their count covered all app-based workers and this one covers drivers.

Third, the federal figure is the outlier in the other direction, and instructively so. The Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries recorded 19 fatal intentional-injury deaths across the entire combined taxi and ridesourcing industry in 2019, a category that includes every traditional cab driver in the country. This study documents 8 app-driver homicides that year. The two numbers are not comparable, and that is the point: the federal system cannot tell you how many of its 19 were app drivers, because the occupational code does not distinguish them.

What the reconciliation establishes: the counts were never far apart, and were never the hard part. Uber knew its driver death toll to the case. Lyft knew. Gig Workers Rising built a defensible list from public records. What none of them produced, and what required going back through ten years of court records case by case, is the other half of every one of these events: who did it, what they were charged with, and what a court did about it.

The dispatch is the mechanism. The most consequential finding in this dataset is that in 53 of 108 homicides with a documented relationship, the killer was a passenger on the fatal trip. In those cases the platform did not fail to prevent a random crime. It performed its core function, matched two strangers, and directed one to the other's location. That is not an argument that the platforms are liable, which is a legal question this study does not attempt. It is an argument that the risk is structural to the product rather than incidental to the neighborhood.

The interracial finding, and why it probably is not about race. Only 40.0% of documented suspect-to-victim pairings here are intraracial, against roughly 80% in national homicide data. That is a large departure and it invites a racial reading. The occupational explanation is simpler and fits the rest of the data better. Most homicide is intraracial because most homicide happens between people who live near each other, and American neighborhoods remain segregated. App driving breaks that pattern by design: it takes a workforce that is heavily immigrant and heavily non-White and dispatches it, by algorithm, into neighborhoods its members do not live in. The encounters are not drawn from a shared community, so they do not inherit that community's demographics. The seventeen-year age gap between victims and the people charged with killing them points the same way. This is not neighbors killing neighbors.

The discrimination literature, and the asymmetry it reveals. There is a well-developed body of peer-reviewed research on ride-hailing discrimination. A 2016 field study across Seattle and Boston, running roughly 1,500 rides, found Black riders waited up to 35% longer and that cancellation rates roughly doubled for riders with African American sounding names, with Black male riders in low-density areas affected roughly three times as often. A 2024 study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences estimated that more than 3% of drivers must be discriminating to produce the observed cancellation gaps, while finding that rapid rematching cut the resulting wait-time penalty to about eleven seconds and that residential segregation independently produced roughly 50% longer waits.

That research is careful and its findings stand. The observation this study adds is about attention, not about its conclusions. Over the same decade, gold-standard field experiments, a PNAS paper, federal interest, and sustained press coverage measured what drivers did to passengers. Nobody measured what happened to drivers. One side of that ledger was funded, peer-reviewed, and covered. The other side produced 151 corpses and no list.

What this data cannot support, stated explicitly. These findings do not establish that refusing passengers by race would protect drivers, and this study does not argue it. The arithmetic forbids it. Uber recorded roughly 2.1 billion trips across 2019 and 2020 against five driver deaths from assault, a risk on the order of one in 400 million trips. No refusal rule keyed to a passenger characteristic can be justified as risk management at that base rate. This dataset cannot connect killer race to any ride-acceptance decision in any case: 75 of 148 charged individuals have no documented race at all, and only 53 of 150 killings were committed by a passenger. Anyone citing this project to argue that drivers were right to profile is citing it against its own evidence.

The refusal question is real, and it is about penalties rather than race. The documented tension is between platform enforcement and driver discretion. Drivers report declining to cancel rides they felt were unsafe because cancellation and acceptance rates carry consequences, and a 2023 survey of rideshare drivers found a majority of drivers of color continued rides despite feeling unsafe. In June 2022, members of Congress wrote to the chief executives of Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Grubhub, and Instacart arguing that no driver should be punished for declining a ride or delivery they believe would place them in danger. Uber has stated that canceling at pickup for safety reasons does not count against a driver's cancellation rate. That is a narrow assurance, and it does not address acceptance rates or pre-pickup declines. The policy question raised by this dataset is whether a driver's judgment about a specific situation carries any cost, not whether drivers should be permitted to screen by demographics.

Half the perpetrators are undocumented, and that is a finding. 75 of 148 charged individuals have no publicly documented race, concentrated among juveniles whose records are sealed and suspects still at large. Any published breakdown that assigns race to those 75, in either direction, is inventing data. The year pages correct several circulating compilations that do exactly that.

The three findings a narrative-driven project would bury are on the page in bold: half the charged population has no documented race, this violence crosses racial lines far more often than American homicide generally, and cases with Black victims are cleared at the same rate as cases with White victims.
The study also refuses the inference that would make it useful to the crudest reading of it. The arithmetic of a one-in-hundreds-of-millions per-trip risk cannot support any refusal rule keyed to a passenger characteristic, and Objection 9 says so directly.

At least 151 app-based drivers were killed working in the United States between 2016 and 2025. 150 of those were homicides. 32 of them have never been solved. In 53 of the cases where a relationship is documented, the person who killed the driver arrived because the app sent them.

The people who died were, on average, 41.9 years old. Many were immigrants. The people charged with killing them averaged 24.6, and nearly one in five was a minor.

Two companies counted these deaths in aggregate and named nobody. One advocacy group named the victims of a single year and then stopped. The federal government cannot separate these workers from taxi drivers in its own data and is not required to try. Every one of those counts was defensible on its own terms, and together they left the central question of every case unanswered for a decade.

The record now exists. It is incomplete, it is a floor, and it will need correction. It is also the first time anyone has written down who did it.

Methodology summary: This index aggregates ten year datasets; every count is computed from the case tables on those pages, and each year page documents its sources case by case. Source coverage falls in three eras: 2016, assembled from contemporaneous local reporting and court records with no institutional list available; 2017 through April 2023, anchored to Gig Workers Rising's Murdered Behind the Wheel database and independently verified and expanded; and mid-2023 through 2025, compiled directly from local news, police statements, prosecutors' offices, and court records. Inclusion: homicide of a driver working for an app-based rideshare or delivery platform, Amazon Flex included, at the time of the killing or in direct connection with that work; killings while working but not because of the work are included motive-blind with circumstances stated; non-criminal traffic accidents excluded. Police-involved killings are counted in victim totals and broken out of homicide statistics. Suspect race is recorded only where publicly documented via booking photo, court imagery, or law enforcement description; unknowns are coded unknown and never inferred. Relationship coding is documented-only and unsolved cases always code Unknown. Solved or Charged counts any homicide with at least one person convicted or charged; cases closed because the killer died are counted separately. No rate per driver, per trip, or per capita is computed anywhere in this project, because no public source records how many app drivers work in a given place or year. All figures verified as of August 2026.
